**A late 1099 for 2026 costs $60 a form if you file within 30 days of the February 1, 2027 deadline, $130 by August 1, and $340 after that or if you never file.** The IRS copy and the contractor's copy are penalized separately, so the real cost is up to twice that. A wrong 1099 is fixed by filing a corrected form and sending the contractor a corrected copy; errors of **$100 or less** don't need fixing at all unless the contractor asks. There is no first-time forgiveness for 1099 penalties, so the cheapest move is always the same one: file now, even late, even imperfect.

This is the post for February and March. If it is still January, you have time: the [ways to file 1099s online](/blog/file-1099s-online-2026) cover the fastest routes.

## The penalties, for forms due in 2027

| When you file the correct form | Per form | Yearly maximum, gross receipts of $5 million or less |
|---|---|---|
| Within 30 days of the due date | $60 | $244,500 |
| After 30 days, by August 1 | $130 | $698,500 |
| After August 1, or never | $340 | $1,397,000 |
| Intentional disregard | The greater of $690 or 10% of the amount | No maximum |

The figures are from Revenue Procedure 2025-32. Businesses with average gross receipts over $5 million have higher maximums.

Two separate sections of the tax code apply: one to the form you file with the IRS, and one to the copy you give the contractor. They use the same amounts and stack.

**A shop's example.** A plumbing shop owes five 1099-NECs on February 1, 2027 and files them, and sends the subs their copies, on February 19. That is within 30 days: 5 forms × $60 × 2 copies = **$600**. In May it finds a sixth sub it missed and files right away: $130 × 2 = **$260**. Had it never filed the sixth, that would be $340 × 2 = $680.

The table is the whole argument for filing late rather than not at all. Every tier is cheaper than the last one.

## Extensions: what exists, and what doesn't

**Filing with the IRS.** Form 8809 gets you 30 more days. For most information returns, including the 1099-MISC, the first extension is automatic. For **Form 1099-NEC it is not**: there is one 30-day extension, and you have to give one of the reasons the IRS lists:

- A federally declared disaster
- A death or serious illness of the person responsible
- A fire, casualty or natural disaster affecting the business
- The first year the business was established
- Not receiving the data needed from payees in time

Request it by the due date, not after. "We were busy" is not on the list.

**Contractors' copies.** An extension to file with the IRS does not extend the date you owe contractors their copies. That is a separate request, on **Form 15397**, sent online or by fax by the date the copies are due. The IRS no longer takes letters for it, and it generally grants up to 30 days.

## A late W-9 doesn't move the deadline

The most common reason a 1099 is late is waiting on a contractor's W-9. The due date doesn't move for that. File on time with what you have, even without a tax ID, and correct it later if you need to.

What protects you from a penalty for a missing tax ID is showing you asked, on the IRS's schedule:

1. **An initial request** when the relationship begins, in writing or verbally
2. **A first annual request** by December 31 of the year you paid them, if you still don't have it
3. **A second annual request** by December 31 of the next year

Once a missing tax ID comes in, you don't have to file a corrected form unless an amount also changes; use the tax ID going forward. And remember that a contractor without a tax ID on file is subject to **24% backup withholding**, starting right away. The [W-9 guide](/blog/collect-w9-from-contractors) has the request emails.

## Small errors you don't have to fix

The IRS has a safe harbor for small mistakes. An error of **$100 or less** in an amount, or **$25 or less** in withholding, doesn't need a corrected return and isn't penalized. The exception: if the contractor asks for a corrected statement, you file a corrected return with the IRS and send them a corrected copy.

A separate rule helps when you catch more than small errors early. If your forms were filed on time and you correct them by August 1, the IRS doesn't penalize the corrections for the greater of 10 forms or one-half of 1% of the forms you filed.

## How to correct a 1099

Every correction has two halves: a corrected return to the IRS, and a corrected copy to the contractor. If your originals had to be filed electronically, the corrections do too.

**In the IRIS Taxpayer Portal.** Forms you filed through the portal are corrected through the portal, from the original once it shows Accepted or Accepted with Errors.

| What's wrong | IRIS |
|---|---|
| Wrong amount, code or checkbox | One-step correction |
| Wrong name or tax ID | One-step correction |
| Filed a form you shouldn't have | One-step correction, with every amount set to zero |
| Wrong form type, say an NEC that should have been a MISC | Two steps: zero out the original, then file a new original |

Each form can be corrected once in IRIS; a later fix goes on that latest correction. A mistake in your own information as the payer can't be corrected in IRIS at all. You write to the IRS instead.

**On paper.** Corrections are by type, each sent with a new Form 1096 for that form type.

| What's wrong | On paper |
|---|---|
| Wrong amount, code or checkbox, or a form filed that shouldn't have been | One form marked CORRECTED, with the right figures |
| Wrong or missing tax ID, wrong name, or wrong form type | Two forms: one marked CORRECTED with the original information and zero amounts, then a new original with the right information |

Don't check the VOID box on a correction; that box is for something else.

## When the IRS writes first

The IRS proposes information-return penalties on **Notice 972CG**, "Notice of Proposed Civil Penalty." It lists the forms it thinks were late, missing a tax ID, or filed on the wrong media, with a response page. Respond within the time it gives, generally 45 days, before the penalty is assessed.

There is no first-time abatement for these penalties. That relief exists for failure to file, pay or deposit on returns like the 1040, not for 1099s. Relief here is by **reasonable cause**: an event beyond your control, or significant mitigating factors, and evidence that you acted responsibly both before and after. A contractor who never answered your W-9 requests counts only if you made the requests on the schedule above. Keep the emails.
