**For payments made in 2026, you send a 1099 to a person or unincorporated business you paid $2,000 or more for services, rent or other reportable payments, by check, ACH, cash or Zelle.** That includes sole proprietors, partnerships, and LLCs that are not taxed as corporations. It generally does not include **corporations**, with three exceptions that catch people every January: **attorneys' fees, money paid through an attorney, and medical payments** are reportable even when the payee is a corporation. Card and PayPal payments are left out, because the processor reports them. The W-9 is how you tell which is which.

The most common question in January is about LLCs, so start there.

## LLCs: the W-9 decides

"LLC" in a vendor's name tells you nothing about how the vendor is taxed. An LLC can be taxed three ways, and each gets different treatment.

| The LLC's W-9 says | Gets a 1099? | Name and tax ID on the form |
|---|---|---|
| Single-member LLC, disregarded | Yes | The owner's name on line 1, the LLC's name on line 2; the owner's SSN or EIN |
| LLC taxed as a partnership | Yes | The LLC's name and EIN |
| LLC taxed as an S corporation or C corporation | No, unless legal or medical | |

A disregarded single-member LLC is reported under its owner, because for tax purposes the owner is the business. The W-9 tells the LLC to give you the owner's Social Security number, or the owner's EIN if the owner has one. An EIN the LLC got only to run payroll is not the number to use; the W-9 is.

On the Rev. March 2024 W-9, the classification is on line 3a. An LLC checks the box and writes in its tax classification: C for C corporation, S for S corporation, or P for partnership. A single-member LLC that is disregarded checks its owner's classification instead.

## Corporations, and the three exceptions

Payments to corporations are generally exempt from 1099-NEC and 1099-MISC reporting. That covers C corporations, S corporations, and LLCs taxed as either.

Three kinds of payment are reportable anyway:

1. **Attorneys' fees** for legal services, on a 1099-NEC, at $2,000 or more. The law firm being a professional corporation doesn't matter.
2. **Gross proceeds paid to an attorney**, like a settlement paid through the other side's lawyer, on a 1099-MISC box 10, at $600 or more.
3. **Medical and health care payments**, on a 1099-MISC box 6, at $2,000 or more, including to professional corporations. Tax-exempt and government hospitals are excluded.

"Inc." on an invoice is a hint, not a classification. Get the W-9 and rely on what it says, not on the name.

## Card, PayPal and Venmo payments

Payments made by credit card, debit card, or through a third-party network like PayPal or Venmo for business are reported by the payment company on Form 1099-K, and they are not reported on your 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC. Leave them out of the contractor's total.

That means one contractor's year can split in two. A sub you paid $1,500 by check and $1,500 by card has $1,500 of reportable payments, under the $2,000 line. A sub paid $2,400 by Zelle has $2,400, because Zelle is a bank transfer and doesn't issue 1099-Ks.

For payment apps and marketplaces, the 1099-K threshold is back to more than $20,000 and more than 200 transactions. Card processors report every card payment. Neither changes your rule: card and app payments are someone else's to report.

## Foreign contractors

A designer in Canada or a developer in the Philippines who works for you from there doesn't get a 1099. Ask for **Form W-8BEN** (for an individual) or **W-8BEN-E** (for a business) instead of a W-9. A foreign person with a valid W-8 is exempt from 1099 reporting, and pay for work done outside the United States isn't U.S.-source income.

Where the work is done is what counts, not where the contract was signed or where you pay. A nonresident who comes to the United States to do the work is reported on **Form 1042-S**, with 30% withholding unless a treaty exemption is claimed on Form 8233. That is a separate set of rules and worth a call to your CPA before the first payment.

## Everyone else

| Payee or payment | Gets a 1099? |
|---|---|
| Sole proprietor or freelancer | Yes, at $2,000 for services |
| Partnership | Yes |
| Your employees | No; their pay is on the W-2 |
| A landlord who isn't a corporation | Yes, 1099-MISC box 1 for rent of $2,000 or more |
| A real estate agent or property manager you pay rent to | No; the agent reports it |
| Supply houses, for parts and merchandise | No |
| Freight, storage, telephone and similar | No |
| Tax-exempt organizations, government agencies | No |
| Personal payments, like your own house's plumber | No |
| Anyone you backup withheld from | Yes, regardless of the amount |

The two lines people get wrong are the third and fourth. North Star's name says LLC, but its W-9 says S corporation. The law firm is a corporation, and it still gets a form.
