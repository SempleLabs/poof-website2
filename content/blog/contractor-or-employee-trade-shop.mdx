**The helper you pay by the day is almost certainly an employee. The licensed sub with his own truck, insurance and other customers, who bids the job and is told only what the result has to be, is probably a contractor.** The IRS decides by control: if you direct only the result, it is a contractor; if you direct how, when and with what, it is an employee. The Labor Department's test leans the same way, and California and a few other states start from the assumption that every worker is an employee. Getting it wrong costs roughly 10.7% of the wages in federal employment tax for a year the IRS reclassifies, before unemployment, workers' comp and overtime. And there is a way to fix it for a tenth of that.

With 1099s due February 1, 2027, January is when this question gets asked. Here is how to answer it for a shop.

## The IRS's three tests

The IRS uses the common-law rules, grouped into three kinds of evidence. No single one decides it; you weigh them together.

| | Points to a contractor | Points to an employee |
|---|---|---|
| **Behavioral control** | Told only what result you need | Told when, where and how to work; trained in your methods |
| **Financial control** | Own tools, truck and insurance; pays own expenses; bids a flat price; can make or lose money; advertises and works for others | Uses your tools and materials; expenses reimbursed; paid by the hour, day or week |
| **Type of relationship** | A contract for a defined job; no benefits; the job ends | Ongoing with no end date; benefits; the work is the core of what your shop does |

The IRS's general rule is short: a worker is an independent contractor if you have the right to direct only the result, not what is done or how. The right is what counts, whether or not you use it.

## The IRS's own construction examples

IRS Publication 15-A works through cases that read like a trade shop's crew.

- **Helpers and assistants** working under a contractor who approves them and inspects the work are employees of the contractor.
- **A worker the company supplies with materials, whose work it inspects, and whom it covers under workers' comp** is an employee.
- **A roofer with a flat-price contract, his own business name and insurance, responsible for fixing his own mistakes**, is an independent contractor.
- **A tradesperson paid a flat amount per job who advertises and takes other contracts** is an independent contractor.

The pattern for a shop: subs who run their own business are contractors. People who work your jobs, your way, on your schedule are employees, whatever you call them.

## Where the Labor Department's test stands in 2026

For minimum wage and overtime, the federal Fair Labor Standards Act has its own test, the economic reality of the relationship. Its status is unsettled:

- **The 2024 rule**, with six factors (opportunity for profit or loss, investment, permanence, control, whether the work is integral to the business, and skill and initiative), took effect March 11, 2024, and is still the regulation on the books. It still applies in private lawsuits.
- **Since May 1, 2025**, the Labor Department hasn't used it in its own enforcement. Investigators use its 2008 guidance instead, which names construction outright as one of the most common places workers are misclassified, and which says a state license has no bearing on the question.
- **In February 2026**, the department proposed replacing the 2024 rule with a version of its 2021 rule, which weighs control and opportunity for profit or loss most. Comments closed April 28, 2026. As of November 2, 2026, there is no final rule.

Under any of these versions, a helper who works your schedule with your tools for an hourly wage is an employee.

## State tests can be stricter

The IRS test isn't the only one that matters. Unemployment insurance and workers' comp are run by the states, and each decides classification under its own law.

**California** uses the ABC test. A worker is an employee unless the business proves all three: (A) the worker is free from its control, under the contract and in fact; (B) the work is outside the usual course of the business; and (C) the worker is customarily engaged in an independent business of the same kind. Prong B is the problem for a shop: an HVAC company hiring an HVAC tech fails it.

California has a **construction subcontractor exemption** in Labor Code section 2781. A sub falls outside the ABC test only if every condition is met:

1. The subcontract is in writing
2. The sub holds a contractor's license covering the work
3. The sub has any local business license or tax registration required
4. The sub has a business location separate from yours
5. The sub can hire and fire its own people
6. The sub takes financial responsibility for its errors, through insurance, bonds or warranties
7. The sub is customarily in an independent business of the same kind

California also makes a license a condition of contractor status: someone doing work that requires a contractor's license must hold one to be an independent contractor. An unlicensed helper doing licensed work can't be one.

**Massachusetts** and **New Jersey** use ABC tests too. New Jersey adopted detailed rules for its test that took effect in 2026. **Texas** decides unemployment-tax classification with a 20-factor common-law test focused on direction and control; a contract or a 1099 doesn't decide it.

## What misclassification costs

If the IRS reclassifies a worker and the mistake wasn't intentional, section 3509 sets reduced rates for the back employment tax: 1.5% of wages for income tax you should have withheld, plus 20% of the employee's share of Social Security and Medicare, plus your full employer share. The IRS puts the total at **10.68% of wages** up to the Social Security wage base ($184,500 for 2026). If you didn't file the 1099s, the first two rates double.

| Three helpers paid $45,000 each in a year | Federal employment tax owed |
|---|---|
| 1099s filed, not intentional | About $14,420 (10.68%) |
| No 1099s filed | About $18,510 (13.71%) |
| Intentional disregard | The full taxes, with no reduced rates |

That is one year, federal only. On top of it:

- **Federal unemployment tax**, normally $42 per employee a year after the state credit
- **State unemployment tax** and any state penalties
- **Workers' comp.** A misclassified worker hurt on a job can file a claim, and the state board decides whether they were an employee
- **Overtime.** An employee is owed time-and-a-half for hours over 40 in a week. A helper on 50-hour summer weeks has back overtime coming
- **Benefits** the employee would have been eligible for

## Two ways out

**Section 530 relief.** If you treated the workers as contractors in good faith, the IRS can't make you pay the back employment taxes when all three are true: you filed their 1099s on time; you never treated anyone in a similar job as an employee; and you had a reasonable basis, such as court decisions or IRS rulings, a past IRS audit of the same workers, long-standing practice in a significant segment of your industry where you work, or advice from a CPA or attorney.

**The Voluntary Classification Settlement Program.** If you want to start treating workers as employees, this settles the past for **10% of the section 3509 tax for the most recent year**, with no interest, no penalties, and no employment tax audit of earlier years for those workers. In the table's first row, that is about $1,442 instead of $14,420.

To qualify, you must have treated the workers consistently as contractors, filed their 1099s for the previous three years, and not be under an IRS employment tax exam or a Labor Department or state classification exam. File **Form 8952 at least 120 days** before the date you want to start treating them as employees. You pay after the IRS accepts and you sign the closing agreement.

The 1099s are the thread through both: filing them on time keeps your options open. That is one more reason to [file them by February 1](/blog/file-1099s-online-2026).

## A shop's checklist

For each person you pay outside payroll, ask:

1. **Do they have their own business?** A business name, an EIN, a license for the work, their own insurance, other customers.
2. **Do they bid the job or get paid by the hour?** A price for a result points to contractor; an hourly or day rate points to employee.
3. **Whose truck, tools and materials?**
4. **Who sets the schedule and the method?**
5. **Is it a defined job, or ongoing with no end?**
6. **Is the work what your shop sells?** In ABC-test states, this one alone can decide it.

Mostly "their own" and "a defined job": a contractor, with a [W-9](/blog/collect-w9-from-contractors) and a 1099-NEC if you paid $2,000 or more in 2026. Mostly "yours": an employee, on payroll. Unsure: ask your CPA, or file Form SS-8 and ask the IRS, which can take six months or more.
